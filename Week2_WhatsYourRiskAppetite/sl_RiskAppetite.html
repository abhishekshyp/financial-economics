The tool · Expected Value03

Expected value is the average you gain (or lose) per play.

Weight each outcome by its probability and add them up. Over many repetitions, this is the number your average wealth gravitates toward.

EV = p·(gain) − (1−p)·(loss)
Decision rule: all else equal, choose the higher expected value — because EV drives your long-run average wealth.

Apply it · the 60/40 coin04

Every flip has positive expected value.

Stake $20 on the biased coin. You win $20 with probability 0.60, and lose $20 with probability 0.40.

EV = 0.60 × (+$20) + 0.40 × (−$20) = +$4

A +$4 expected gain on every $20 staked — a +20% edge. On average, each flip adds to your wealth.

So the "rational" answer looks obvious: keep flipping until you hit the $250 cap.
Expected wealth — the theory
Betting $20 a flip, expected profit rises by $4 every time. Straight up and to the right.
The reality05

And yet — 28% went bust.

In the real experiment, roughly one-third of participants ended with less money than they started with. Startling, for a coin you were told was 60/40 in your favour.

More astonishing still: 28% went bust and received no payout at all. A game with an ex-ante 60/40 winning edge wiped out more than a quarter of its players.

Participant outcomes
Same edge for everyone; wildly different endings.
"How did you go bankrupt? Gradually, then suddenly."
— Ernest Hemingway, The Sun Also Rises, 1926
Why did a winning game lose money?06

A 60% edge on every flip — so why did so many lose, or go bankrupt?

01

Positive EV doesn't guarantee success

A positive expected value is necessary but not sufficient for long-term success. One good average hides many ruinous paths.

02

Overbetting destroys wealth

The goal is not to maximise the return on a single bet, but to maximise wealth over a lifetime. Stake too much and one bad run ends the game.

03

Percentage losses hurt more than gains help

A −50% drawdown from ₹100 leaves ₹50 — and now needs a +100% gain just to break even. Losses and gains are not symmetric.

Does this carry into real markets? Yes.
The recovery asymmetry
The gain needed to erase a loss grows faster than the loss itself.

Barber & Odean (2000), "Trading Is Hazardous to Your Wealth", Journal of Finance: the investors who traded the most earned the lowest returns. Overactivity is overbetting.

A quiet assumption07

Why could we even compute an expected value?

Because we knew everything that mattered. In the ELM game, nothing was hidden:

  • The probability of winning — 60%
  • The probability of losing — 40%
  • Exactly how much we would gain
  • Exactly how much we would lose
  • That the rules would never change
This is called risk.
Knight, 192108

Risk versus Uncertainty

Risk — measurable

  • Probabilities are known
  • Outcomes can be estimated
  • Expected value can be calculated

Uncertainty — immeasurable

  • Probabilities are unknown
  • Outcomes cannot be estimated accurately
  • Expected value is often impossible to calculate

The distinction was drawn by economist Frank H. Knight in 1921: risk is measurable; uncertainty is not.

Last week we computed a lifetime total income. That number is an expected value — but whether you actually achieve it is uncertain.

e.g. over-leveraged firms in a downturn

Equity trades in seconds with visible depth; property can take months.

One bad event wipes out a large share of your wealth.

e.g. ESOPs + salary — human & financial capital on the same firm
The one split that matters10

Every risk falls into one of two buckets

Systematic (market) risk

  • Affects the whole market at once
  • Cannot be diversified away
  • e.g. interest rates, recessions, war, oil shocks

Unsystematic (specific) risk

  • Affects one firm or sector
  • Can be diversified away by holding many names
  • e.g. a CEO exit, a product recall, a failed trial

If it's measurable — measure it11

Two investments, same 10% average. Which do you take?

Averages hide the journey. Standard deviation measures how widely returns scatter around that average — small spread, low risk; large spread, high risk. Finance always reads return and risk together.

Correlation risk

Two stocks in the same industry tend to fall together. The higher their correlation (ρ), the less a 50/50 mix cushions you — diversification only works when things don't move in lockstep.

Now measure the investor12

Can you put a number on your own risk tolerance?

Method 1 — the questionnaire

A short psychometric quiz. You answer a series of questions, each option scored a / b / c, and the totals map to a risk profile.

How the score is built

Count your answers in each category, then weight them:

score = (na × 1) + (nb × 2) + (nc × 3)

A higher score = greater willingness to bear risk.

Measure the investor · Method 213

Method 2 — revealed preference

Don't ask people what they feel — read what they actually hold. Given a risk-free rate and a risky asset, the optimal risky weight is y* = (E[r] − rf) / (A·σ²). Invert it, and the portfolio someone chose reveals their risk-aversion coefficient A.

Back out A from a portfolio
rf = 7%, E[r] = 13%, σ = 20% (σ² = 0.04)
A = (0.13 − 0.07) / (0.20 × 0.04) = 7.50

Managing personal risk · II15

Diversification runs along many axes

You don't diversify once — you diversify in every direction a shock can come from.

Across geographies
India, USA, Japan, emerging markets — no single economy owns your outcome.
Across asset classes
Equity, debt, real estate, gold — they respond to different forces.
Across sectors
Financials, IT, FMCG, energy — within equity itself.
Across market caps
Large, mid, small, and early-stage — different risk and growth.
Each axis strips out a different slice of unsystematic risk. What remains — systematic risk — is the market risk you're paid to bear.
Managing personal risk · III16

Your biggest asset isn't in the market — it's you

Every one of us carries a personal risk with a 100% probability: we will die. The only question is when.

When you're young, your human capital — the present value of all your future earnings — is your largest asset (recall last week's human-vs-financial-capital crossover). Your family lives off that future income.

If it disappears overnight, who pays the rent? Who supports your parents? Term life insurance converts your human capital into a payout the moment it's lost — for a tiny premium. It insures the asset you can't diversify.

Rule of thumb: cover ≈ the value of your human capital.
